How the reading was made

I read the story closely first, stated where I was reading from, then asked a local language model to extract and score themes under the same framework.

  1. Partial readingThree blocks, each 20% of its third of the story: the first block, a random block and the last block.
  2. Initial impression and positionAn impression, my position as a reader, a research problem and three theoretical frameworks.
  3. Text uploadThe whole story, cleaned of page headers, ornaments and scan errors.
  4. Query generationA structured prompt, run in AnythingLLM on LM Studio.
  5. Targeted extractionA Python dictionary of five themes with scores and evidence, checked against the story.
  6. Computation and visualisationTheme vocabulary counted in each tenth of the story to test three statements.
  7. InterpretationThe counts read through literary and cultural theory, below.

Close reading

Opening lines

“John is a physician, and perhaps … that is one reason I do not get well faster. You see he does not believe I am sick!”
“So I take phosphates or phosphites—whichever it is, and tonics, and journeys, and air, and exercise, and am absolutely forbidden to ‘work’ until I am well again.”

Later lines

“At night in any kind of light … it becomes bars! The outside pattern I mean, and the woman behind it is as plain as can be.”
“I’ve got out at last … in spite of you and Jane! And I’ve pulled off most of the paper, so you can’t put me back!”

Initial impression

The lines show a severe tension between institutional authority, medical and marital, and a woman’s own experience. Her confinement under the name of therapeutic rest becomes a projection onto the wallpaper, and spatial entrapment ends in a radical break from domestic obedience.

Positionality

I read “The Yellow Wallpaper” as a student of Humanities and Social Sciences at IIT (ISM) Dhanbad with a sociological background, trained to read texts as evidence of institutions, roles and power. I came to the story through feminist standpoint epistemology and the medical gaze. What I noticed first was how John’s authority as both husband and physician decides what counts as the narrator’s illness. My position may lead me to overlook the story’s literary form, such as its irony and unreliable narration, and the details of nineteenth-century medicine. Counting vocabulary also reduces the story to word lists.

Research problem

How does institutional paternalism pathologize female creative autonomy as illness, and how does the domestic interior become a site of epistemic violence and counter-hegemonic madness?

Five themes and their intensity

The LLM scored each theme from 1.0 to 10.0 for textual weight and narrative trajectory. These are estimates, not measurements; the counts below test where each theme actually sits. Open a theme for its analysis and evidence.

Three statements, tested by counting

Before counting, I wrote three statements about where each theme should sit. I then cleaned the scanned text (6,225 words), split it into ten equal tenths and counted theme vocabulary per 100 words with count_themes.py.

  • Confinement and medical language is denser in the first half. Supported: 0.45 against 0.22 hits per 100 words for confinement, and 0.74 against 0.16 for medical language.
  • Creeping-double language grows towards the end. Supported: the rank correlation with position in the story is 0.87.
  • Liberation language is concentrated in the final fifth. Supported: 0.56 hits per 100 words in the last fifth against 0.06 in the first four-fifths.

Interpretation

Medical language is heaviest in the first tenth (1.93 per 100 words) and is gone from the seventh tenth onwards, apart from one hit at the very end. In Foucault’s terms the story opens inside the clinical gaze: diagnosis, tonics and regimen are reported as settled facts. Hartsock’s standpoint theory and Spivak’s idea of epistemic violence explain why this matters, because the narrator’s own account is overruled by a husband who is also her doctor (“You see he does not believe I am sick!”). As her attention moves to the wallpaper, her own looking replaces the clinic’s.

Creeping-double vocabulary is almost absent until the middle and peaks late, in the eighth tenth (1.93). This fits Gilbert and Gubar’s reading of the woman behind the paper as the narrator’s double, a figure who becomes clearer as the narrator identifies with her.

Liberation vocabulary stays near zero until the final tenth (0.96), where the narrator peels the paper, says she has “got out at last” and creeps over her fainted husband. Gilman’s own account of the story as an attack on the rest cure makes this the reversal of the opening: the medical gaze is now looked at from outside. Whether the ending is freedom or collapse is what the theme title leaves open. The counts show only that the rupture arrives at the very end. Confinement language also returns in the last tenth (0.80), when she locks the door and throws away the key, so its fall in the first half is not a steady decline.

Limitations

The scan has errors that my script fixes only in part, so some matches are missed. Each tenth holds between 0 and 12 hits, so the trends matter more than any single bar. The word lists are mine, built from the verified evidence and key terms, and some words (work, woman, free) have other uses. The 1–10 scores come from one run of one model and are estimates.
